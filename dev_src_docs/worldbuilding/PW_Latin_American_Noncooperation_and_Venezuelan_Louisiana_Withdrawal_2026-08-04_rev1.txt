1. PURPOSE

This page extends the Latin America critical-minerals shadow-contest source with two direct consequences of the earlier Crodyn-era American clandestine campaign:

- a meaningful number of Latin American governments refuse to assist the United States after the PANDORA attacks and the subsequent invasion because earlier aggressive American covert action on their territory has destroyed trust and created durable political resentment;
- Venezuela goes beyond noncooperation, joins the invading coalition, attacks the Gulf Coast of Louisiana during the diegetic interval between Game 1 and Game 2, fails to establish an adequately sustained foothold, exhausts the resources required for continued direct military action, and withdraws its forces from United States soil and the associated coastal waters.

Venezuela's withdrawal ends the failed Louisiana expedition but does not end its political alignment with or indirect support for the wider coalition invasion and occupation effort.

This is a background world-history and war-event lock. It does not by itself create a playable mission, campaign ladder, operation title, exact date, order of battle, or final Louisiana battlefield map.

The event may potentially be depicted in the Project Warriors: Semper Fi DLC, but no placement in that DLC is locked by this source.

2. RELATIONSHIP TO THE EARLIER AMERICAN CAMPAIGN

The earlier American critical-minerals campaign is remembered not only by cartels, criminal intermediaries, and private commercial actors. It also leaves a durable state-level political legacy.

Governments whose territory, institutions, security services, political systems, or commercial sectors were penetrated or pressured by American clandestine action may view Washington's post-PANDORA requests for help through the memory of:

- operations conducted on their soil without transparent consent or durable accountability;
- manipulation of local security priorities around American mineral and corporate interests;
- selective support for favored political, commercial, security, or criminal actors;
- coercive pressure against governments or officials who resisted American access;
- economic disruption and local instability produced by raids, interdictions, sanctions, covert financing pressure, or partner-force activity;
- the perception that Washington treated regional sovereignty as conditional whenever strategic resources were involved.

The result is not a single coordinated Latin American policy. It is an uneven but strategically important refusal by multiple governments to rescue a United States that had previously demanded access, alignment, and compliance through clandestine pressure.

3. POST-PANDORA NONCOOPERATION LOCK

After PANDORA and during the opening phase of the invasion, a meaningful number of Latin American governments refuse American requests for assistance.

The exact country list and the exact form of each refusal remain open unless later dedicated sources specify them. Depending on the government and moment, noncooperation may include refusal or restriction of:

- military basing or staging access;
- overflight and transit permission;
- port access and emergency naval support;
- intelligence sharing;
- logistics, fuel, maintenance, and resupply support;
- emergency diplomatic alignment;
- policing or disruption of coalition-linked movements and networks;
- expedited commercial, medical, or commodity support.

These are possible forms of the broader lock, not a declaration that every refusing government denies every category.

Some governments remain formally neutral. Some condemn PANDORA while refusing to support the American war effort. Some provide limited humanitarian help but deny military cooperation. Some hedge between the United States, China, regional blocs, domestic factions, and the invading coalition. Others exploit American weakness to renegotiate old political and commercial relationships.

The refusal should not be written as irrational spite. It is the strategic consequence of a history in which American officials expected regional governments to accept coercive intervention when the United States was strong and then expected automatic solidarity when the United States became vulnerable.

4. VENEZUELA'S ESCALATION

Venezuela does not merely refuse assistance.

Venezuela joins the foreign invading coalition and commits forces to an attack against the Gulf Coast of Louisiana.

The operation takes place sometime after the events of Game 1 and before the events of Game 2. Its exact position within that diegetic interval remains open.

The exact political authorization, coalition command relationship, force package, route, landing points, operational name, and date remain open. The controlling facts are:

- Venezuelan forces participate as an active member of the invading coalition;
- their assigned or chosen operational focus is the Louisiana Gulf Coast;
- the operation attempts to establish a usable foothold on United States territory;
- the operation occurs inside the Game 1-to-Game 2 interval while the coalition invasion and occupation are already reshaping the United States.

The operation may later be developed through naval, amphibious, airborne, covert, proxy, or mixed components, but no specific insertion method is locked by this page.

5. FAILURE TO SECURE THE LOUISIANA FOOTHOLD

The Venezuelan operation does not produce a durable occupation zone.

Venezuelan forces fail to secure an adequate foothold on land. They cannot establish the protected depth, reliable port or beachhead function, local control, and sustainment architecture required to keep the intervention viable.

The failure is materially driven. The contingent runs short of the resources needed to continue operating effectively and cannot replace losses or maintain the tempo required to consolidate its position.

Potential later development may define the resource problem through some combination of:

- fuel depletion;
- ammunition expenditure;
- maintenance and spare-parts failure;
- inadequate sealift or resupply protection;
- insufficient engineering capability;
- medical and casualty pressure;
- loss of transport or command assets;
- coalition prioritization of other fronts;
- effective American military, Coast Guard, National Guard, local-defense, or irregular resistance;
- severe weather, geography, infrastructure damage, or contested waterways.

These are open authoring possibilities. This source does not lock one precise failure chain.

6. WITHDRAWAL LOCK

After failing to secure a sustainable foothold and running out of the resources necessary to continue direct military action, Venezuela withdraws its deployed forces from:

- United States soil; and
- the coastal waters associated with the Louisiana operation.

The withdrawal ends Venezuela's direct Louisiana ground and littoral presence and its ability to sustain an independent expeditionary campaign against the United States.

It does not constitute a Venezuelan political break with the invading coalition, a repudiation of the invasion, or a transition into neutrality.

What is locked is narrower and explicit: Venezuelan forces do not retain a durable occupied foothold in Louisiana and do not remain indefinitely in the adjoining operational waters.

7. CONTINUING INDIRECT COALITION SUPPORT

After the Louisiana withdrawal, Venezuela remains supportive of the coalition's wider invasion and occupation of the United States.

Its reduced material position prevents it from sustaining another comparable direct expeditionary campaign, but it continues contributing through indirect and lower-cost means. These include:

- fuel supplied to other coalition members and coalition-linked logistics systems;
- covert financing and financial-channel support;
- mercenaries, contractors, proxies, or deniable personnel in limited numbers;
- cyber operations and cyber-support activity;
- intelligence, communications, facilitation, procurement, transport, documentation, or sanctions-evasion assistance;
- other indirect forms of support that help coalition members sustain invasion, occupation, reinforcement, or coercive activity.

The exact scale, recipients, routes, agencies, companies, intermediaries, cyber units, financial mechanisms, and mercenary identities remain open.

Venezuela's contribution after withdrawal should be represented as persistent but materially constrained. It remains politically committed and operationally useful to the coalition without possessing the resources to resume a large independent conventional campaign on United States territory.

Fuel support does not imply limitless Venezuelan capacity. Covert financing does not imply Venezuela alone funds the occupation. Limited mercenary or proxy support does not restore a standing Venezuelan occupation zone. Cyber activity may continue at a lower cost than expeditionary warfare but remains subject to later mission- and capability-specific development.

9. STRATEGIC CONSEQUENCES

The failed Louisiana operation produces several useful worldbuilding consequences.

For the United States:

- it demonstrates that the invasion is geographically broader than the most durable occupation zones;
- it creates an early defensive success without erasing the national catastrophe;
- it may preserve or reopen parts of the lower Mississippi and Gulf logistics environment, subject to later mapping;
- it shows that coalition members have unequal capabilities, sustainment depth, and political commitment;
- it does not fully remove Venezuela from the war because Venezuelan resources continue reaching other coalition actors indirectly.

For Venezuela:

- the withdrawal can become a domestic political humiliation, a concealed defeat, or a contested official narrative;
- lost personnel and equipment can weaken the regime or intensify internal repression;
- coalition partners may regard Venezuela as a failed expeditionary actor but still value its fuel, financing, cyber, proxy, and facilitation support;
- the state remains committed to the coalition while shifting toward less resource-intensive forms of participation;
- China may later exploit the failure through finance, reconstruction, political leverage, or intelligence access without having directed the original operation.

For Latin America more broadly:

- the episode divides governments that chose neutrality or noncooperation from a government that entered the war directly;
- it prevents regional resentment toward American clandestine action from becoming a single uniform ideological alignment;
- it gives later governments reasons both to fear American return and to distrust coalition promises;
- it helps create the fragmented postwar environment in which Chinese and residual American networks compete for influence.
