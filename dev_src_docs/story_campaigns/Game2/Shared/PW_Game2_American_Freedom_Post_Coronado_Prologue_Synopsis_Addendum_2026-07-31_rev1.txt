UPDATED PROLOGUE SYNOPSIS
After the defense of North Island, Romeo’s record from the European withdrawal, the AEGIS infrastructure campaign, the return to occupied California, and the Battle of Coronado is reviewed by surviving senior U.S. command. His valor and repeated ability to preserve routes, work across units, and lead under degraded command bring him to the attention of a compartmented special-operations and interagency planning cell supporting resistance inside occupied American territory.

Romeo is not promoted into a heroic national role or publicly celebrated. He remains a staff sergeant. The change is that he is admitted into a smaller circle of trust and given an assignment that depends on judgment rather than conventional frontage.

The planners show him decrypted Russian occupation communications and traffic-analysis summaries from eastern Kentucky. The intercepts reveal a low but persistent pattern of unexplained supply losses, communication disruptions, and local movement around Calloway. Russian personnel suspect that isolated incidents may reflect organized resistance, but neither side has identified a mature network. U.S. command does not know who is responsible, whether the activity is secure or legitimate, or whether it can survive sustained counterintelligence pressure.

Romeo is tasked to enter the occupied area covertly, establish contact with the suspected cell, assess its people and security, and help develop communications, logistics, intelligence handling, routes, and operational discipline. He is not sent to impose outside command or launch an immediate liberation offensive. The mission gives him time and latitude to help credible local actors become a durable partner while preserving civilian legitimacy.

Fire Team Delta does not accompany the opening insertion. Romeo begins Game 2 separated from Dingo, Howler, and Beaver, preserving their later reunion in HIGH GROUND and forcing him to build trust without relying on his familiar Ranger team.

The briefing also establishes the larger strategic clock. Mobile surviving U.S. formations are assembling for a counteroffensive that advances east from California along a more southerly route toward the Mississippi, crosses the river, and then turns northeast toward the Appalachian and Virginia approaches. Romeo’s local mission and the conventional advance are presented as complementary plans: the mobile force will eventually provide mass, engineering, aviation, and sustained logistics, while the eastern-Kentucky resistance can provide intelligence, routes, local access, and a legitimate human network inside occupied terrain.

The prologue ends with Romeo accepting the task and beginning the covert movement toward Calloway. LOW GROUND retains the uncertainty of his arrival and the need to discover whether the resistance activity described in the briefing is real.

The two plans converge late in the campaign. By TRI-STATE POINT, the resistance Romeo helped develop has become the American Freedom Corps, and forward elements of the wider U.S. advance have reached the Cumberland region. Their joint liberation of the Cumberland Gap corridor pays off the prologue’s two-line strategy and enables larger formations to continue toward southwestern Virginia and farther northeast.
